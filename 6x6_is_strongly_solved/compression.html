The databases up to 12 pieces

How well do the databases compress?

Measured with compression_stats on the 10-piece layer.

The distribution

Of all positions with 10 pieces 44.4% are won, 29.6% lost and 26.1% drawn for the side to move. In 78.0% of the positions the side to move can capture. Their value follows from one ply of captures (into databases with fewer pieces), so a compressor may store anything there: here the value of the previous position is filled in, so that the runs get longer. 40.4% of the slices have a single value, but they are small: together 0.76% of the positions.

What this means for 12 pieces

With the capture positions filled in, xz reaches 0.173 bits per position, a factor 12 below 2 bits. At that rate the databases with 11 pieces take some 0.5 GB and those with 12 pieces 0.5 GB; up to 12 pieces together 1.4 GB. That fits in memory with room to spare.

Two caveats. For fast lookup the blocks have to be smaller than 1 MiB (a few KB), which compresses somewhat less; and every position with a capture costs one ply of captures when it is looked up. On the other hand the capture positions can be filled in more cleverly than with the previous value, for example with the value that extends a run on both sides.

Small blocks cost little: zstd -19 reaches 0.210 bits per position at 4 KiB, hardly more than the 0.180 at 1 MiB. With small blocks zstd beats xz (0.258), which only wins with large blocks. A dictionary helps a little, and only for the smallest blocks. How fast lookup then is, is shown in the next section. With zstd -19 and blocks of 4 KiB all databases up to 12 pieces take about 1.7 GB; with the faster zstd -3 2.0 GB. Both fit in memory with room to spare.

How fast is lookup?

Measured with compression_lookup: the compressed blocks and a block index are in memory; a lookup finds the block, decompresses it and extracts the value (every result checked against the original), 2 million lookups per measurement. Random: every lookup a random position, so every time a block is decompressed. In runs: 64 positions close together (within ±2,000 positions), with the last decompressed block as a cache, as when a slice is built, where the successors of nearby positions lie close together. Without a cache decompression decides everything: the time grows with the block size, from some 3 µs at 1 KiB to 45–50 µs at 64 KiB (about 0.7 GB per second), and four cores scale almost perfectly. zstd -19 decompresses as fast as -3 and is smaller; only compression is slower, and that happens once. With a cache a lookup takes some 0.5 µs, about 2 million per second per core. A good choice looks like blocks of 4 KiB with zstd -19: 0.210 bits per position (about 1.7 GB up to 12 pieces), some 6 µs for a random position and 0.5 µs with a cache. Not yet counted: a position with a capture costs one ply of captures when it is looked up, so one lookup per capture in a smaller database.

Lookup through mmap

Measured with compression_mmap: the files are opened with mmap. Warm: every page is already in the operating system's page cache. Cold: the file is first evicted from that cache, so the first touch of a page reads it from disk (measured over the first 100,000 lookups; this is a virtual disk in the cloud, on a local SSD it may turn out differently). Random positions, every value checked.

Through mmap, lookup in the compressed blocks is as fast as in ordinary memory: decompression decides the time, and the file is small enough to be fully cached soon. A cold page costs a few microseconds extra here. The uncompressed file is some 200 times faster when warm (a few tens of nanoseconds, just a memory access), but also 10 times larger: up to 12 pieces 16 GB against 1.7 GB. A possible middle way: the databases that are looked up most often uncompressed, the rest compressed.

Lookups while building

Measured with build_lookups: 68,176 slices with 10 pieces (1 in 200, 91,842,918 positions) solved again with the engine's solver, counting every lookup outside the slice (captures, man moves, promotions) and mapping it to its 4 KiB block; the results are checked against the stored values. Per position there are 2.05 lookups: 1.87 in the first pass and 0.18 when checking the loss candidates. They are highly concentrated: a slice touches only 11.2 different blocks on average. With a cache of 256 blocks (1 MB per core) that is roughly 1 µs per position: for the 45.5 billion positions with 11 or 12 pieces some 3 hours on 4 cores, the price for storage that is 2.7 times smaller.

With a cache of 64 blocks (256 KB per core) 99.4% of the lookups are served from the cache, and lookup costs some 0.17 µs per position. For the 45.5 billion positions with 11 or 12 pieces that is roughly 2 hours on one core: compressed lookup is no bottleneck. Two caveats: the 10-piece slices are small (1,350 positions on average), and with compressed storage a looked-up position with a capture still costs one ply of captures, which raises the number of lookups.
